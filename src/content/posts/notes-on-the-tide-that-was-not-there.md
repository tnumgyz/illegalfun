The estuary at Weldenmouth is a wide flat nothing at low water — three miles of
grey silk with a channel cut through it, and a wind that arrives from the sea
without anything to break it. I had gone out at dusk to walk the sea wall, which
is a mile of concrete with a rail on one side and nothing on the other, and I had
intended to turn back at the sluice.

I did not turn back at the sluice. By then the water had begun its trick.

## The sound arrives first

A tide announces itself before it appears. There is a hiss, very low, and under
the hiss a knocking as the first of it finds the shingle. I knew this sound. I had
walked that wall a dozen times and the sound was the reliable part, the part that
told you how long you had.

The sound came in on schedule. The water did not.

I want to be careful here, because the obvious version of this is that I could not
see in the dark, and that is not what happened. There was enough moon to read the
mud by. I could see the channel, and the ribs of the old jetty, and the pale line
where the last tide had left its wrack. All of it stayed where it was. And over it
came the sound of a tide making, at volume, from a great width — a sound that
requires several million tons of water and had none.

## Being a witness

What I felt was not fear. It took me most of an hour to work out what it was, and
the closest I have come since is this: I felt *dismissed*. As though the estuary
had been doing something for a long time and had now decided it could do it
without the part of the arrangement that involved being seen.

I stood at the rail and did the reasonable things. I checked my watch, which was
working. I looked for the boats at Craill, which were where they should be, with
their two lights. I put my hand flat on the concrete and it was cold and slightly
damp and entirely concrete. Every instrument I had reported normally, and reported
into a roar of water that was not arriving.

There is a particular loneliness in having your senses disagree in public. Alone
in a room you can put the disagreement away. Out on a wall in the open, with a
landscape that is plainly a landscape and plainly lying, I had the strong sense of
being an unnecessary witness at an event that had gone on for ten thousand years
and had never once required me.

## The turn

Around what I think was eleven the sound began to go out again. It receded the way
a real ebb recedes, with the knocking dropping away first and the hiss thinning
after it, over perhaps forty minutes. The mud, which had not been covered, was
uncovered anyway. By the time it was quiet the flats were exactly as they had been
at dusk, and I was very cold, and my hands had stopped working properly on the
rail.

I walked back. At the landward end there is a bench with a plaque on it for
someone who liked this view, and I sat on it for a while because my legs had
opinions about the last mile. The plaque says the person was *never happier than
here*, which I read four or five times.

## Afterward

I have been back to Weldenmouth twice since, both times in daylight, both times
with the tide doing its ordinary work. It comes in over the flats in a thin sheet
faster than a person walks, which is the fact the signs warn you about, and it
makes the sound. Water and sound together, as advertised.

I am not looking for an explanation, and I would not know what to do with one. What
I took from the sea wall was smaller and stranger than an explanation: a working
knowledge of what it is like to be told, courteously and at length, by a place you
are fond of, that the arrangement does not depend on you.
